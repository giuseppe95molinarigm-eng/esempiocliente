The Shape of Ordinary Days

Chapter One · The Lower Shelf

An excerpt from an imagined memoir, written to show how we work with voice, scene and dialogue when a book is drawn from lived experience. The narrator returns home after a long stay in the hospital; the chapter follows their first day and the morning after.

The key stuck the way it always had, half a turn and then noth­ing, and Nell said, “Let me,” and I said, “I’ve got it,” and for a while we stood on the front step with my hand on the key and hers held an inch above it, not quite touching.

The trick is to lift the door by the handle as you turn. I had known that for eleven years. It took me three tries to re­mem­ber it.

Inside, the first thing was the smell. Not a bad one—ra­di­a­tor dust, the cedar block in the coat closet, some­thing faint­ly like or­anges—just the smell of my own house, which I had ap­par­ent­ly never no­ticed while I was living in it. I stood in the hall and breathed it in and felt, ab­surd­ly, like a guest.

“Do you want to sit down?” Nell said.

“I’ve been sit­ting down since February.”

“That isn’t an answer.”

It wasn’t. There was a bench by the door that hadn’t been there before, a low pine thing with a cush­ion tied on at the cor­ners, placed ex­act­ly where I would stand to take off my shoes. I looked at it. Nell watched me look at it.

“It was forty dol­lars,” she said. “You’re al­lowed to hate it.”

“I don’t hate it.”

I sat on it. My shoes were the ones I’d been wear­ing the day I left, and some­body—Nell—had cleaned them. The laces were new. I undid them slowly, partly be­cause my hands were slow and partly be­cause I didn’t want to stand up again just yet in front of her, and she knew that, and went into the kitchen to make a noise with the kettle so I could do it with­out an audience.

That is the kind of sister she is. It is also the kind of sister she is that she called out, “Take your time!” from the kitchen, which undid most of the good.

The house had been kept beau­ti­ful­ly, and it was not quite mine.

The mail was stacked on the hall table in four bun­dles, one for each month, each with a rubber band and a yellow sticky note: February, March, April, May. The plant on the win­dowsill—a pothos I had been fail­ing to kill for years—had been wa­tered so faith­ful­ly that it had put out a new vine and trained it along the cur­tain rail. The fridge held foods I don’t buy: oat milk, a bag of washed spinach, little pots of yogurt with names like Orchard. The cal­en­dar on the kitchen wall still said February. Nell hadn’t turned it. I didn’t know whether that was tact or su­per­sti­tion, and I didn’t turn it either.

“I’ll stay till the end of the month,” Nell said. She had her back to me, pour­ing water. “Work’s fine about it. Graham’s fine about it.”

“You don’t have to.”

“I know I don’t have to.”

“I just mean you’ve already—”

“I know what you mean.”

What I meant, I think, was two things at once. I meant: go home, back to your own kitchen and the hus­band you’ve been talk­ing to on speak­er­phone since winter, so that I can find out what I’m like when nobody is watch­ing. And I meant: please don’t. I couldn’t have said which was louder. She put a cup of tea in front of me, in a white mug she must have brought from home, and sat down across the table with hers, and we drank them, and nei­ther of us said any­thing else about it.

My friend Theo called at four.

“You’re home,” he said. “How is it? How are you feeling?”

“Good,” I said. “Really good. It’s strange, but a good strange. It’s very quiet.”

All of that was true, the way a post­card is true. I was sit­ting on the bottom stair be­cause it was the near­est place to sit when the phone rang, and while we talked I had been look­ing at the other thir­teen and think­ing about them in a way I had never thought about stairs. Not with fear. More the way you might look at a long sen­tence in a lan­guage you used to speak well.

“You sound tired,” Theo said.

“I’m on the stairs,” I said, as if that ex­plained it, and he laughed, and I laughed, and he told me about a man at his office who had start­ed bring­ing a stand­ing desk to meet­ings, and I said all the right things in the right places. When we hung up I stayed where I was. Then I went up, one hand on the rail, and at the top I sat down on the land­ing carpet, which is not a thing adults do in their own homes, and waited until I was ready for what­ev­er came next.

Nell found me there on her way to the bathroom.

“Oh,” she said.

“I’m just sitting.”

“Okay.” She stepped over my legs, care­ful­ly, and then stopped and came back and sat down beside me, her knees up near her chin the way she used to sit when we were chil­dren watch­ing tele­vi­sion through the ban­is­ters. She didn’t ask any­thing. After a minute she said, “This carpet is dis­gust­ing, by the way,” and I said, “I know,” and that was, some­how, the best con­ver­sa­tion I’d had all day.

The mugs were the thing that went wrong.

It was early evening. Nell was in the back­yard on the phone to Graham, walk­ing up and down the path, and I de­cid­ed I would make the tea. It seemed a man­age­able am­bi­tion. I filled the kettle—the kettle was where it lived—and switched it on, and then I reached up to the cab­i­net above the toast­er, where my mugs had been since the day I moved in, and opened it, and found cans of soup.

The mugs were in the cab­i­net under the counter, on the lower shelf, lined up in two rows with their han­dles all facing the same way. So were the plates. So were the teapot and the honey and the tea itself. Everything I used every day had come down to waist height, and every­thing I didn’t—the soup, the good glass­es, a fondue set some­one gave me years ago—had gone up.

I stood there with the cab­i­net door open for long enough that the kettle clicked off.

“You moved the mugs,” I said when she came in.

She looked at the open cab­i­net, and then at me, and I watched her decide how to answer.

“I moved the things you use.”

“I didn’t ask you to.”

“No,” she said. “You weren’t really in a po­si­tion to be asked.”

It came out sharp­er than she meant. I saw it reach her own face a second after it reached mine. She put her phone down on the counter.

“Sorry. That was—”

“It’s fine.”

“It isn’t. I just thought, the first few weeks, you wouldn’t want to be stretch­ing up for things. I don’t know. I read something.”

“I can reach a cab­i­net, Nell.”

“I know you can.”

Neither of us knew that. That was the prob­lem, un­der­neath the mugs. I didn’t know yet what I could do. For months, people had told me what I could do—sit up, stand, walk to the window, walk to the end of the cor­ri­dor and back—and now I was home, where no one was going to tell me any­thing, and the first thing I’d found was that my sister had al­ready guessed. She had guessed low. She might have been right.

Nell made the tea in the end. I let her.

That night I couldn’t sleep, which every­one had warned me about, though not in a way that helped. The bed was too wide and the room was too dark, and the quiet pressed on my ears. For months I’d had a door that never quite closed, voices pass­ing at all hours, a light in the cor­ri­dor that stayed on. I had hated all of it. Now I lay in my own bed and missed it, and felt ridicu­lous, and then felt some­thing un­der­neath the ridicu­lous­ness that I didn’t want to look at, and got up.

The land­ing light was on. Nell’s door was half open and I could hear her talk­ing, low, the way you talk at mid­night to some­one who is also in bed.

“I don’t know,” she was saying. “Says everything’s fine. Says it all the time.” A pause. “No. No, I’m not going to push. I’m just going to be here, that’s all. I’m going to be in the kitchen.”

I went back to bed before she could hear the floor­board out­side the bath­room, which has creaked for as long as I’ve lived here and which I have never once re­mem­bered in time.

In the morn­ing I was up first.

In May the light comes into the kitchen at an angle, across the table and up the side of the fridge, and I stood in it for a while in my socks. I didn’t feel better, ex­act­ly. I felt like some­one who had slept four hours and would find out later what kind of day this was going to be. I had been told there would be good days and bad ones, and that the only way to know which was which was to get to the end of them.

I filled the kettle. I opened the cab­i­net under the counter and took two mugs from the lower shelf: my blue one with the chip in the rim, and Nell’s white one from home. I put the milk in hers first, which I have always said is bar­bar­ic. I didn’t put it in mine. Then I turned the cal­en­dar to May.

She came down at half past seven with her hair flat­tened on one side, saw the mug wait­ing at her place, and lifted it and looked at the color of it and said, “Milk first, you animal,” and kissed the top of my head on her way to the toaster.

Afterward I washed both mugs and dried them and stood for a moment with the cab­i­net door open. Then I put them back on the lower shelf, han­dles facing the same way, where I would find them tomorrow.
